Prologue · 130 million light-years away

The sky is changing faster than we can watch it.

Somewhere in the universe tonight, something will explode, collide or be torn apart. Its light reaches us once, and then it fades. This is the story of teaching ten thousand satellites to decide, together, where to look.

Scroll to travel
I · 17 August 2017 NGC 4993

Two dead stars collided, and the universe rang like a bell.

130 million years ago, two neutron stars spiralled into each other. The ripple in spacetime crossed the void and, on a summer morning in 2017, shook the LIGO and Virgo detectors on Earth.

1.7 seconds later, a burst of gamma rays arrived. Telescopes on every continent turned to look. In the glowing debris they saw heavy elements being forged, gold among them.

II · Every flash is a clock

Then the light starts to fade.

The debris splits in two. A fast blue wind races out from the poles and fades within days. A slower red cloud, heavy with gold and platinum, glows for weeks.

Every kind of event fades at its own pace, and the shape of that fading tells you what you are looking at. Miss the window and the question closes for good.

IV · Who looks?

When the sky flashes, which one should turn its eye?

The bright star rising over the limb sits where NGC 4993 really is: RA 13h 09m, Dec −23°. For about half the fleet the Earth is in the way. Some are low on power. Links between them blink on and off as they pass each other, so no single satellite sees the whole picture.

V · How it works today

Today, they hold an auction.

OrbitalNet OS runs the Contract Net Protocol. A mission is posted. Each orbital plane elects a leader. Satellites that pass the hard checks bid a score made of geometry and battery charge, and the best bid wins.

Live bids from satellites in view that can see the event. Battery levels are simulated.

It is fast, and it is fair. It never asks what the observation would teach us.

VI · Differential diagnosis for the sky

A good doctor doesn’t order every test.

They order the one test that best separates what is still possible. The planner in this research does the same. Before each look it asks which measurement, taken when, would most reduce our uncertainty about what the event is. That is the expected information gain. The light is fading, so a later look is worth less.

A sketch of the idea on synthetic data, computed live as you scroll. Each new point fires a satellite's beam. Not an experimental result.
VII · The unknown h₀

Sometimes it is none of the above.

Most planners only know the classes they were given. Show them something new and they force it into the nearest box, with full confidence.

This one keeps a seat open for an explicit unknown, called h0. When the data stops fitting anything it knows, it switches goals. It stops asking which known thing this is, and starts asking what this is.

VIII · A sky that watches itself

The next great discovery may last a few hours. We want to be looking.

OrbitalNet began as a live simulation of satellites bidding for work. It is becoming a way for a constellation to reason about what it sees, together, before the light is gone.
