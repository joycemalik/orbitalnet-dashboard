OrbitalNet

What should a satellite do when it sees something it has never seen?

That one question has kept us busy for a year. We started with satellites that could share work between themselves, then slowly worked our way up to satellites that can look at a new flash in the sky and say, honestly, that it does not fit anything they know.

Nov 2025 – Feb 2026 · Auction

Who takes the job

It began in November 2025 as a sketch on paper, then a few trial systems to see what would hold up. By February we had one that worked. Each satellite bids for a ground task, the bid scores how close it is and how much battery it has left, and the best bid wins, so nobody on the ground has to assign the work.

It worked well, but it only ever answered who should do the job. It had no idea which measurement was worth doing in the first place, and that gap is where the rest of this story comes from.

    Apr 2026 · Live twin

    Every orbit, live

    Then we wanted the sky to be real, so we pulled in all 10,109 Starlink orbits from public data and moved them forward in time with SGP4, the same model the trackers use. A physics engine, an auction engine and this globe all talk through one message bus, so any part can crash and come back without taking the others down.

    We showed it live on 13 April. It looked great, but underneath it was still choosing by distance and battery, and the science question was still missing.

    Oct 2026 · h₀ planner

    Room for the unknown

    So we changed what a satellite is trying to do. Now it picks the measurement that best tells the possible explanations apart, and we gave it one extra explanation, h₀, which simply means "none of the above". When a flare comes in that fits nothing it knows, each new look pushes its belief towards h₀ instead of towards the least bad wrong answer.

      Nov 2026 · Fleet

      A fleet that talks

      One satellite is not how this would really fly, so we spread the planner over eight of them that share what they have seen whenever they are in view of each other. Sharing helped, but where the satellites sat mattered even more.

      ptsof accuracy lost when the whole fleet sits in one orbital plane

      We also gave the word "unknown" a false-alarm rate that holds at every step, which came out at on fresh events. Realistic links on their own did not beat satellites working alone, and the paper says why.
